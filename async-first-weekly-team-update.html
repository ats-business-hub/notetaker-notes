How to Run an Async-First Weekly Team Update

An async-first weekly update means the default way you share progress is a written status, sent on a fixed day, not a standing meeting that exists to hear the same information out loud. It does not mean “never meet.” It means you write first, you give decisions a close date, and you book a call when writing has already shown that a call is needed.

This is a process guide for small teams, especially remote or hybrid ones. It is not a claim that async always works, and not a hands-on test of Slack, Teams, or any AI notetaker. If you still hold calls you want captured, use the tool pages separately — for example the best AI meeting note takers guide, the Microsoft Teams guide, or the Laxis review.

A cadence that can hold

Pick three times and write them down, with timezones:

  1. When the weekly status is due. One day, one time. People should not have to guess whether “end of week” means Friday afternoon or Monday morning.
  2. When written decisions close, if they were opened during that week and were not given a custom window. A mid-week close leaves time to extend a date, or to book a meeting, before the next status is due.
  3. An optional live slot, used only when a decision is blocked, a walkthrough is required, or the topic is sensitive. If nothing needs the slot, cancel it in writing and point back to the statuses and memos.

The status itself can follow the weekly async status template on this site. Open choices belong in Decisions needed, or in a decision memo, not in a heading called “discussion.” Keep a decision log so last month’s yes or no is not stuck in a chat thread.

When to write, when to meet

Write first when the facts can be read, the decision can wait for a written window, the people involved share enough context (or the memo will include it), and the topic is not sensitive in the ways below.

Meet anyway when:

  • The decision is blocked on a disagreement you have already written down once
  • Someone will be surprised, blamed, or put at risk — including conversations about performance, conflict, health, or legal risk
  • You need a whiteboard, a walkthrough, or a negotiation
  • You do not yet know the question you are asking

If you convert a memo into a meeting, write that down, with a time. After the call, do not leave the official record only in a long thread. File the notes, and close the decision in the memo and the log. For turning a notes draft into owners and dates, see turning AI meeting notes into action items.

Worked example (fictional)

The team, people, and calendar below are invented for this page. They are not a client story and not a report of a real newsroom.

Lumen Press is an eight-person editorial group. Four people work in Asia/Tokyo and three in Europe/Lisbon; one freelancer moves between the two. They used to hold a 45-minute “weekly sync” on Thursday that often repeated what was already in chat. A written cadence could look like this:

Fictional weekly cadence for Lumen Press
When What happens
Monday, 10:00 Asia/Tokyo Each desk (or each person, if the desk is a single editor) sends a weekly status: goal, done, next, decisions needed, risks. Lisbon is Sunday evening, so those statuses can be written Friday or Sunday; they are still due by this Monday cutoff.
Monday–Wednesday Comments go on the status or on a linked memo, not as a new standing meeting. Required reviewers are named in the memo.
Wednesday, 17:00 Asia/Tokyo (08:00 Europe/Lisbon) Default close for written decisions opened that week, unless a memo set a different window. If a required reviewer is out, the author extends the date in writing.
Thursday, 16:00 Asia/Tokyo (07:00 Europe/Lisbon), 25 minutes, optional Used only when a memo says “meeting scheduled,” or when a walkthrough cannot be written. If the slot is empty by Wednesday evening, Noor cancels it in the same channel and links the closed or still-open memos.

A fictional open item in that cadence: Noor El-Sayed wants to hold the December theme issue to 18 pages instead of 24. That is a decision, not a status bullet. The memo names options (18, 24, or a 20-page middle), a recommendation (18, to fit the already-slipped illustration schedule), required reviewers (Tomé Silva and Erin Blake), and a close of Wednesday 17:00 Asia/Tokyo. If Tomé disagrees and the same points have already been written once, Noor books the Thursday slot and records that the memo was converted to a meeting.

What this example does not claim: it does not say Lumen Press saved a number of hours, and it does not say every team should copy these exact clock times. The useful part is that the due time, the close time, and the optional meeting are written down, with timezones, before the week starts.

Start with one week

You do not need a new tool to try this. Pick a due day, copy a status template, and agree that standing “updates” meetings are optional for two weeks. Keep the meetings you still need for walkthroughs and hard conversations. At the end of the second week, look only at facts you already have: which statuses arrived, which decisions closed in writing, and which calls you still had to add. Do not invent a productivity percentage. Then keep, adjust, or drop the cadence in writing — which is itself a short decision memo.

FAQ

Does async-first mean we should cancel all meetings?

No. Write first for status and for decisions that can wait for a written window. Meet when a disagreement is already written down, when someone may be surprised or put at risk, or when you need a walkthrough or a negotiation.

What times should we pick?

Times that include a timezone, and that the whole team can name without guessing. The fictional example on this page uses a Monday due time and a Wednesday close because that group spans Tokyo and Lisbon. Your clocks can be different. Write them down before the week starts.

How do we know if the cadence is working?

Look at facts you already have after a couple of weeks: which statuses arrived, which decisions closed in writing, and which calls you still had to add. Do not invent a productivity percentage.
